Robust Verification

Producing a correct answer turns out to be easier than knowing which answer is correct — and closing that distance is what makes an agent able to improve itself.

Ask a language model a hard question once and it will often get it wrong. Ask it the same question a hundred times and, surprisingly often, at least one of those hundred attempts is right. The right answer is already there, sitting in the pile. The problem is that nobody knows which one it is.

Everything in this lesson follows from that one asymmetry. If you had a perfect judge — something that could look at a hundred attempts and reliably point at the good one — you could turn a mediocre model into a strong one without retraining it at all. You would also have something more valuable: a source of feedback the model could learn from, over and over, without a human in the room. That judge is what researchers call a verifier, and this lesson is about how to build one you can actually trust.

Why picking is harder than producing

The previous lesson established that spending more compute at test time works: sample many candidate solutions instead of one, and the odds that a correct solution appears among them climb steadily. Researchers call this coverage — the chance that at least one attempt in a batch is right.

Coverage is only half a method. To turn it into an answer you have to choose, and choosing badly wastes everything the sampling bought you. Three things can do the choosing, and each has a different failure mode.

A tool with ground truth. Run the code and see whether the tests pass. Check the proof in Lean. These verifiers are close to perfect, and they cover a narrow slice of what we want agents to do. A human. Accurate and broad, and impossible to scale to millions of judgements. Another model. Cheap, general, endlessly available, and wrong often enough to be dangerous.

The rest of this lesson is essentially one long attempt to make the third option behave more like the first two.

2

The first verifiers: train a model to grade

The idea has a clean origin. In 2021, Cobbe and colleagues at OpenAI released GSM8K, a set of 8.5 thousand grade-school maths word problems written to be linguistically varied but conceptually simple. Even the largest models of the day did poorly on them, which was the point: the problems isolate multi-step reasoning from knowledge.

Their fix was to train a second model whose only job was to judge whether a completion was correct. At test time, generate many candidate solutions and return the one the judge ranks highest. It worked, and the more interesting finding was about scaling: verification improved with additional data faster than simply fine-tuning the generator on the same data.

The idea worth keeping
Judging an answer is a smaller job than producing one. So a modest verifier can lift a much larger generator, and the compute you spend on judging often buys more than the same compute spent on training.

This style of verifier grades the finished product. It is now usually called an outcome reward model, and the next section is about the specific way it goes wrong.

3

A right answer is not a right method

Suppose a model makes an arithmetic slip early on, then makes a second slip later that happens to cancel the first, and arrives at the correct number. A verifier that only sees the final answer marks this a success. Worse, if you use that verifier as a training signal, you have just taught the model that the broken reasoning was good.

These false positives are not rare, and they get more common as problems get longer. The response, from Lightman and colleagues in Let's Verify Step by Step (2023), was to move the feedback inward: grade every intermediate step rather than the final result. That is process supervision, and the model doing it is a process reward model.

The results were decisive. Process supervision clearly beat outcome supervision on the harder MATH dataset, and their process-supervised model solved 78% of problems in a representative subset of the MATH test set. Active learning — choosing which solutions are worth having a human label — made the labelling budget go considerably further.

The catch is in the sentence you just read. Those labels came from people. The team released PRM800K, 800,000 step-level human judgements, which is an extraordinary gift to the research community and an obviously unrepeatable way to build verifiers for every new domain.

4

Getting step-by-step labels without humans

Math-Shepherd (Wang et al., 2023) asks a simple question: what would it mean for a step to be good? A reasonable answer is that a good step leaves you in a position from which the problem can still be solved. And that is something you can measure by experiment.

Take a partial solution up to some step. From that exact point, let the model continue to completion many times over. Count how many of those continuations reach the correct final answer. That fraction becomes the step's label. No human reads anything.

Trained this way, the verifier does two jobs. Used at test time to rerank candidates, and used during training as a step-level reward signal, it lifted a Mistral-7B model from 77.9% to 84.1% on GSM8K and from 28.6% to 33.0% on MATH; adding reranking on top brought those to 89.1% and 43.5%.

The constraint that remains
This trick needs a reliable check on the final answer, because that is what tells you whether a rollout succeeded. In maths and code you have one. In writing a research summary, an incident report, or a legal argument, you do not.
5

When nothing can check the answer

This is where most real agent work lives, and it is the subject of Shrinking the Generation-Verification Gap with Weak Verifiers (Saad-Falcon et al., NeurIPS 2025), a paper from the group teaching this course.

What you have available in the open-ended case is a collection of weak verifiers: language models prompted to act as judges, reward models trained on preference data, small classifiers. Each one is individually mediocre. They disagree with each other. They output scores on incompatible scales — one gives a probability, another a rating out of ten, another a bare yes or no. Some are actively unhelpful on a given task.

The obvious move is to average them, and the paper's first finding is that averaging is the wrong move. Verifiers differ substantially in accuracy, so an unweighted vote lets the poor ones drag down the good ones. Weighted combinations do much better — but weights are normally learned from labelled data, which is exactly what you do not have.

Weaver's answer

The framework, Weaver, borrows from weak supervision: you can estimate how accurate each verifier is by looking at their patterns of agreement and disagreement, without ever seeing a correct answer. Around that core it adds two practical repairs — normalising the mismatched output formats using dataset statistics, and filtering out verifiers that are not pulling their weight.

The headline result is a statement about where capability can come from. Using Llama 3.3 70B Instruct as the generator and an ensemble of judge and reward models of 70B parameters or smaller as verifiers, Weaver reached 87.7% average accuracy across the reasoning and maths tasks tested. That is comparable to o3-mini, and it is roughly the size of the jump from GPT-4o to o3-mini — a jump that took extensive fine-tuning and post-training to achieve.

Running a whole committee of 70B judges for every candidate is expensive, so the paper closes the loop by distilling the ensemble's combined scores into a single 400M-parameter cross-encoder. The committee is used once, to teach; the small model does the work afterwards.

6

What makes a verifier robust

Reading the four papers together, a shape emerges. Verification methods trade off along one axis: the more certain a verifier is, the narrower the range of tasks it applies to.

Four things to carry forward

  • Verification is the bottleneck on self-improvement. An agent that improves by generating, filtering, and training on its own output can only get as good as its filter. Sharpen the verifier and every downstream loop gets better at once.
  • Where feedback lands matters as much as whether it is right. A single verdict at the end of a long chain is a weak teaching signal; a verdict per step tells the model what to change.
  • Correlated mistakes are the danger in ensembles. Combining verifiers helps because their errors are partly independent. Verifiers built from the same base model, or from the same preference data, fail together — and a confident consensus of wrong judges is worse than one honest judge.
  • Any verifier used for training becomes a target. Optimise hard enough against an imperfect judge and the generator learns to satisfy the judge rather than the task. This is why the strongest available check, however narrow, should stay in the loop.
The open question
Every method here is grounded, eventually, in a domain where answers can be checked. Extending robust verification to work that has no answer key — research, design, judgement, taste — is still unsolved, and it is where a great deal of current effort is going.
