How AI learned
to improve itself

Making models bigger used to be the only way forward; now the gains come from letting a model think longer, act in the world, and check its own work.

This first lecture is a map, not a lesson. Over about an hour the two instructors walk through the last eight years of AI progress and show that it came in four distinct pushes. Each push worked brilliantly for a while, ran out of room, and was replaced by a push in a different direction. Their claim is that we are living in the fourth one, and that its limit is already visible.

Nothing here requires knowing how a model is built. If you can accept that an AI system is trained on a lot of text and then answers questions, you have enough to follow the whole argument. The words the lecture leans on — scaling, post-training, test-time compute, agent, verifier — are explained as they arrive, and collected again at the bottom.

The argument in one picture

Four pushes, four ceilings

Read the curve left to right. Every coloured segment is a period when one idea was making AI visibly better. Every dashed flat stretch is that idea running out of room. Progress restarted each time not by doing more of the same thing, but by spending effort somewhere new.

Push one · 2018–2024

Make it bigger

For six years the recipe was almost embarrassingly simple. Add more text, add more computer chips, add more internal settings for the model to tune — and the model gets measurably better. Not sometimes. Predictably, along a smooth curve you could plot in advance. These are the scaling laws, and they are why model sizes went from a few hundred million adjustable parameters in 2018 to something in the trillions by 2024.

Size bought two things beyond a better score. The first was learning from examples in the prompt: instead of retraining a model for each new job, you just show it two or three examples of what you want and it copies the pattern. The second was stranger — abilities that are simply absent in smaller models and appear once the model crosses a size threshold.

The standard example is chain of thought. Ask a model a word problem about tennis balls and it may get it wrong. Show it one worked example where the reasoning is written out step by step, and a large model starts writing out its own steps and gets it right. Show that same example to a model roughly a tenth the size and nothing happens at all. That threshold is a large part of why laboratories kept spending on scale: not for the predictable gain, but for the unpredictable one waiting on the other side.

Push two · 2022

Teach it to be useful

ChatGPT reached a million users in five days, and the model underneath was not dramatically bigger than what already existed. What changed was everything layered on top of the raw model — the post-training stack.

The last layer is the interesting one. Rather than telling the model the right answer, people are shown two of its answers and asked which is better. Those preferences train a second, smaller model — a reward model — whose only job is to score answers. That scorer then nudges the main model. The score can be split into parts and weighted: is it correct, is it helpful, is it specific, is it harmless.

Hold onto one detail: every step here edits the model in advance. Once training stops, the model is fixed. That assumption is what the next push breaks.

Push three · 2024–2025

Let it think longer

Here is the pivot of the lecture. You can make a model dramatically better at the moment you use it, without changing a single thing about the model.

Mirhoseini's lab showed this with an experiment named after the infinite monkey theorem. Take a small, cheap, open model. Instead of asking it a hard coding problem once, ask it ten thousand times. Then run the unit tests on every answer and keep one that passes. A model far weaker than the leading commercial system on a single try beats it once you let it sample enough times.

The instructors' reading of this result: a model knows considerably more than a single answer reveals. Asking once samples one path through everything it could have said.

Then reasoning models closed the loop. Instead of you generating ten thousand answers and picking, the model was trained to do the searching internally — to explore several lines of reasoning and land on the good one first time. Give it more time to think and the answer improves, on the same steady curve, with no extra size.

Why this is called self-improvement

Thinking longer produces something valuable as a by-product: enormous quantities of correct, worked-out reasoning on problems where the answer can be checked — maths with known solutions, code with tests. That output becomes training material for the next model. The model's own extra effort turns into the next model's education, and the loop closes.

Looking at the reasoning traces these models produce, the same moves keep recurring: state the problem, break it into pieces, try something, check it against feedback such as a test result, correct the error, and back up to a different approach when stuck. Are those behaviours taught or did they emerge? The instructors are honest that nobody is certain — probably some of both.

Push four · 2025 onward

Let it act, and check

The lecture draws the line cleanly. A chatbot receives a question and returns an answer. An agent receives a goal, plans how to reach it, does things in some environment, looks at what happened, corrects itself, and decides for itself when the job is finished — or reports that it cannot finish it.

A useful corrective follows. Most systems sold as agents are not open-ended loops at all — they are hand-drawn flowcharts assembled from a small vocabulary of parts: calls to a model, tools, searches, verifiers, and judges. The common arrangements are worth knowing by name.

Chaining

Output of one step becomes the input of the next, in a fixed order.

Routing

Something looks at the request and sends easy ones to a cheap model, hard ones to an expensive one.

Split and gather

Many workers run at the same time, then one merges the results. This is how deep-research tools work.

Manager and workers

A central model plans the job, hands out pieces, and assembles what comes back.

The sharpest working example is the coding agent, which reaches the computer through the terminal: move around the repository, search files, read lines, edit them, run a command, read what it printed, decide the next edit. The instructors are candid about timing — this loop was not reliable a year ago and is only now becoming so. What changed was not the idea but stronger models trained with rewards that could actually be checked.

The jobs where it lands hardest are the repetitive ones: migrating code between versions, restructuring, moving data between warehouses, writing tests. Also customer support, and long research write-ups. At the speculative edge sit research agents that suggest experiments and draft papers — and the instructors make a genuinely interesting defence of a model's tendency to invent things, since having read the entire web it will propose directions a specialist would never consider.

The limit already in view

Checking is harder than guessing

The most valuable part of the hour comes from a student question. If a model can produce endless plausible attempts, what stops the self-improvement loop from running forever? The answer: knowing which attempts were any good.

Generating is cheap. Verifying is not. Where a machine can check the work — a maths problem with a known answer, code with a test suite, a rule that either holds or does not — the loop spins and compounds. Where the only real judge is a person, as in creative writing, the loop stalls at the speed of human attention. This is the generator–verifier gap, and the course gives an entire lecture to it.

Two further admissions are worth recording, because both are the kind of thing that usually gets smoothed over. Models appear to learn better from their own attempts than from the attempts of a stronger model. And on whether recent gains come from the reinforcement learning or from richer training data underneath it, the instructors say plainly that there is no consensus yet — both are doing work.

Where the rest of it goes

Each theme above becomes its own session, generally built around three or four recent papers rather than a textbook. The shape of the term:

  • 02–03Test-time compute in detail, then robust verification — the bottleneck, straight after the idea that creates it.
  • 04–05Learning from tool and code feedback; multi-step reasoning and planning.
  • 06–08Scaling reinforcement learning at training time, self-improving systems that evolve their own designs, and search-driven research agents.
  • 13–14Agents for software engineering, and giving agents memory.
  • 17Evaluating agents on long-horizon tasks, which is its own unsolved problem.
  • GuestsSpeakers from Google DeepMind, Reflection AI and Physical Intelligence on post-training, mathematical reasoning, autonomy and robotics.

Marks are split roughly half homework, half project. Teams of two to four do original research with API credits provided; a new benchmark, a reliability study of an agent system, or a serious attempt to beat an existing benchmark all count. A survey paper or a wrapper app does not. Auditing is not permitted, but lectures are posted publicly — which is why you can watch this one.

Worth remembering

Six things to carry out

  1. Scale bought two different goods: a predictable drop in error, and unpredictable new abilities that only switch on above a size threshold.
  2. ChatGPT's advantage over what came before was the post-training stack, not raw size — good writing, worked instructions, then human preferences turned into a scoring model.
  3. Thinking time is a separate lever from model size. Enough attempts plus a way to check them can lift a small model above a much larger one.
  4. Self-improvement is that loop closed: checkable problems generate their own high-quality training material for the next model.
  5. An agent is a chatbot plus a goal it keeps, an environment it can change, feedback it acts on, and a stopping decision of its own. Most shipped agents are still hand-drawn flowcharts.
  6. Verification, not generation, sets the ceiling. Fields where a machine can mark the work compound; fields where only a person can judge do not.

The five words

Plain definitions

Scaling law
The observed rule that error falls along a smooth, predictable curve as you add training text, computing power, or model size.
Post-training
Everything done to a model after it has read the internet, to make it follow instructions and behave the way you want.
Test-time compute
Effort spent at the moment of answering — more attempts, longer reasoning — rather than during training.
Verifier
Anything that can say whether an answer is right: a test suite, a known solution, a rule, or a person.
Agent
A system given a goal that plans, acts on its environment, reacts to what happens, and decides when it is finished.
