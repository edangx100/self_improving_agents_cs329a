Test-time compute scaling

A model gets better at a hard question when you let it try more than once — and past a certain point, buying more attempts beats buying a bigger model.

Where the compute goes

Building a language model costs an enormous amount of computation, and almost all of it is spent before anyone asks a question. Once the model exists, the usual habit is to ask it something, take the first answer it gives, and stop. That single answer is a remarkably small amount of work compared to what went into making the model.

This lesson is about the other place computation can go: the moment of use. Instead of asking once, ask ten times, or two hundred and fifty times. Or ask once, then hand the answer back and say "check this and fix it." Or generate many answers and have the model judge between them. All of these cost more per question, and all of them make the model measurably better without changing a single weight.

The reason this matters for a course on self-improving agents is that an agent which can spend more effort on a hard problem, notice its own mistakes, and try again is doing the most basic version of improving itself. Everything later in the course — verifiers, search, reinforcement learning on its own attempts — is built on the machinery introduced here.

Just try again

The simplest possible way to spend more compute at test time is to run the model repeatedly on the same prompt and collect the answers. Because sampling is random, the attempts differ. The question is how much you gain.

The measure to watch is coverage: the fraction of problems where at least one of your attempts is correct. It is also written pass@k, where k is the number of attempts. Coverage is not the same thing as accuracy — it assumes you can somehow tell which attempt is the good one, an assumption we come back to in a moment.

Brown and colleagues measured this across models and benchmarks, and the pattern was consistent: coverage keeps climbing as you add samples, across four orders of magnitude. Their headline case is a software engineering benchmark. DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct solves 15.9% of SWE-bench Lite issues with one sample and 56% with 250 samples, beating the single-sample state of the art of 43% — which was held at the time by considerably stronger frontier models.

The relationship is often close to log-linear and is well described by an exponentiated power law, which is what makes people call it an inference-time scaling law. It behaves like a scaling law: predictable, smooth, and extendable over a wide range.

It also changes the arithmetic of cost. Holding the surrounding agent framework fixed, sampling the cheaper DeepSeek model five times solved more issues than a single sample from Claude 3.5 Sonnet or GPT-4o, at more than three times lower cost. And under a fixed FLOP budget the best split differs by task — on MATH, coverage is maximised by a smaller model taking more samples; on CodeContests, by fewer samples from a larger one. Model size and attempt count are two dials on the same budget, and neither one is always the right dial to turn.

Finding the good one

Here is the catch that shapes the rest of the course. Coverage counts a problem as solved if any attempt was correct. But in a real system, you have to hand back exactly one answer. If you cannot tell which of your 250 attempts is the right one, high coverage buys you nothing.

In some domains this is free. Code can be run against tests. A formal proof can be checked by a proof assistant. Wherever answers verify automatically, coverage converts directly into performance, and the SWE-bench result above is real performance, not a hypothetical ceiling.

Elsewhere it is the hard part. On tasks without an automatic checker, the standard selection methods — taking the majority answer, or scoring candidates with a learned reward model — plateau beyond several hundred samples and stop tracking the sample budget. You keep paying for attempts, and coverage keeps rising, and the answer you actually return stops improving.

Why this is the pivot of the whole course

Once you accept that generating good candidates is easy and recognising them is hard, a lot of research directions fall out of it: better verifiers, process-level scoring that checks each reasoning step rather than the final answer, weak verifiers combined into strong ones, and using verified attempts as training data. Lesson 3 goes straight at it.

Wide or deep

Repeated sampling is only one shape that test-time compute can take, and it is the dumbest one: every attempt starts from scratch, knowing nothing about the attempts that failed. Snell and colleagues separate two mechanisms.

Parallel. Draw many independent answers and search among them, ideally scored by a process reward model — a verifier that grades each step of the reasoning rather than only the final result, so that search can be steered partway through.

Sequential. Let the model see its own previous attempt and revise it. Here the compute is spent going deeper on one line of thinking rather than opening new ones.

The finding is that neither shape wins outright. Easier questions benefit from purely sequential compute, while harder questions do best at some ratio of sequential to parallel, and the ideal ratio depends on both the budget and the question. That makes intuitive sense: if your first attempt is roughly on track, polishing it is efficient. If your first attempt was in the wrong neighbourhood entirely, polishing it just produces a well-edited wrong answer, and you need to start somewhere else.

Spend by difficulty

If the best strategy depends on how hard the question is, the natural move is to estimate the difficulty first and then choose. That is the compute-optimal strategy: allocate test-time compute per prompt rather than applying one fixed recipe to everything.

Difficulty here is estimated by the model's own machinery — binning questions by how a verifier scores early attempts — so no ground-truth labels are needed at test time.

Matching the strategy to the question is worth a lot. Compute-optimal allocation improves the efficiency of test-time scaling by more than four times compared to a best-of-N baseline — the same accuracy for roughly a quarter of the inference cost, purely from deciding where to spend.

Thinking instead of training

Now the provocative question. Given a fixed number of FLOPs, are you better off pretraining a bigger model, or keeping a small one and letting it think longer?

The comparison Snell and colleagues run is FLOPs-matched: a small model with extra test-time compute against a model roughly fourteen times larger. On problems where the smaller base model already achieves somewhat non-trivial success rates, test-time compute can outperform the 14× larger model.

The qualifier is doing real work. If the small model essentially never gets a problem right, extra attempts have nothing to amplify — there is no signal to select from, and no partially correct draft to revise. Capability that is absent cannot be bought back at inference time.

Test-time compute amplifies what a model can already sometimes do. It does not install abilities the model lacks.

There is a second qualifier about volume. Pretraining is paid once; inference is paid on every query forever. When the expected number of inference tokens is large relative to the pretraining budget, the arithmetic swings back toward training the bigger model. A research prototype answering a thousand hard questions and a product answering a billion easy ones should make opposite choices.

Searching the design space

By this point there are a lot of moving parts: how many samples, from which model, ranked how, merged or not, critiqued or not, checked how. These compose, they interact, and nobody knows the best arrangement for a given task. Archon's proposal is to stop guessing.

It defines an extensible design space covering generation ensembling, repeated sampling, ranking, fusion, critiquing, verification and unit testing, and turns building an LLM system into a hyperparameter optimisation problem. Give it the available models, the available techniques and a compute budget, and a search procedure finds an architecture for your benchmark.

The searched architectures are strong: across MT-Bench, Arena-Hard-Auto, AlpacaEval 2.0, MixEval, MixEval-Hard, MATH and CodeContests, they beat frontier models including GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet by an average of 15.1 percentage points.

The failure modes are as instructive as the wins. The framework works well with models of roughly 70B parameters or more; with 7B open models the best configurations fall well behind single larger models — small models can rank, but they critique and fuse badly, and a pipeline of weak judges is worse than one decent answer. On instruction-following benchmarks, extra layers of critics, rankers and fusers stop helping past about thirty inference calls, while math and coding keep improving with more calls when generated unit tests are used to evaluate candidates. The same pattern from earlier repeats: where you can check answers, compute keeps paying.

Why the curve is a power law

Return to the coverage curve. It looks like a power law, and that raises a puzzle worth taking seriously, because the obvious mathematics says it should not.

Take a single problem the model solves with probability p on any given attempt. If attempts are independent, the chance that k attempts all fail is (1−p) multiplied by itself k times. That decays exponentially — very fast, and much faster than any power law. This prediction holds empirically at the level of individual problems. So where does the slow, straight aggregate line come from?

The answer is in the mixture. Different problems have wildly different values of p, and when the distribution of single-attempt success probabilities is heavy tailed — a small fraction of problems with extremely low success probabilities — those problems warp the aggregate into a power law even though each individual problem is still decaying exponentially.

This is not only a curiosity about curve shapes. It has a practical payoff and a warning.

The payoff: if the aggregate is a mixture, then estimating the distribution of per-problem success rates lets you forecast the whole curve from a modest number of samples. The distributional method predicts the power law exponent with an order of magnitude lower relative error, equivalently using two to four orders of magnitude less inference compute, and it also explains cases where the observed curve deviates from a clean power law. Deciding whether a large sampling run is worth it stops being a gamble.

The warning: a smooth aggregate curve can hide a population that is splitting in two. Most of your gains may be arriving from problems that were nearly free all along, while the genuinely hard tail stays untouched no matter how much you spend.

What to do with this

Compressed into rules you could act on tomorrow:

If this is trueThen
You can check answers automatically Sample hard. Coverage becomes accuracy, and the curve keeps going for a long way.
You cannot check answers Expect selection to plateau in the low hundreds of samples. Spend your effort on the verifier, not the sample count.
The model gets it partly right Revise sequentially. Deep beats wide when the first draft is in the right area.
The model is lost Go wide, and accept that some problems are outside what inference compute can rescue.
You have a fixed FLOP budget Compare a small model with many attempts against a large model with few. The winner depends on the task, so measure it.
You will serve a very high query volume Lean back toward the bigger model. Test-time compute is a recurring bill.
You are choosing a pipeline Search the configuration rather than guessing it, and check your component models are strong enough to critique and merge.

What this lesson leaves open

  • Selection is the bottleneck. Everything downstream of the generator is weaker than the generator.
  • Knowing when to stop. Spending 250 samples on a problem the model was never going to solve is pure waste, and current systems are bad at telling.
  • Deciding difficulty without a verifier, since the compute-optimal recipe depends on an estimate that itself needs scoring machinery.
  • Whether attempts can stop being independent — an agent that genuinely learns within a session rather than re-rolling the dice.

Words used here

Test-time compute
Computation spent while answering, as opposed to computation spent building the model. Also called inference-time compute.
Coverage, or pass@k
The fraction of problems where at least one of k attempts is correct. An upper bound on what a perfect selector could deliver.
Best-of-N
Generate N answers, score them, return the top one. The standard parallel baseline.
Majority voting
Return the answer that appears most often across attempts. Needs answers that can be compared for equality, so it suits short final answers, not essays or patches.
Verifier
Anything that judges whether an answer is correct — a test suite, a proof checker, or a trained reward model. An outcome verifier judges the final answer; a process verifier judges each step.
Revision
Feeding an attempt back to the model and asking for an improved version. The sequential counterpart to sampling more.
Compute-optimal scaling
Choosing the test-time strategy and budget per question according to its estimated difficulty, rather than applying one recipe to everything.
Generation–verification gap
The distance between how well a model can produce a correct answer somewhere in a pile and how well it can identify which one that is.

The four assigned papers

Large Language Monkeys: Scaling Inference Compute with Repeated Sampling

Brown, Juravsky, Ehrlich, Clark, Le, Ré, Mirhoseini, 2024. The coverage scaling result and the cost argument. Start here.

Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally can be More Effective than Scaling Model Parameters

Snell, Lee, Xu, Kumar, 2024. Search against process verifiers, sequential revision, difficulty-adaptive allocation, and the FLOPs-matched comparison against a 14× larger model.

Archon: An Architecture Search Framework for Inference-Time Techniques

Saad-Falcon and colleagues, 2024. Inference-time technique composition as a searchable design space, plus a careful ablation of what each module contributes.

How Do Large Language Monkeys Get Their Power (Laws)?

Schaeffer, Kazdan, Hughes, Juravsky, Price, Lynch, Jones, Kirk, Mirhoseini, Koyejo, 2025. Resolves the exponential-versus-power-law puzzle and turns it into a cheap forecasting method.
